Xiongnu names in Later Han

What the Chinese characters in the Shiji, Hanshu and Hou Hanshu actually sounded like when they were written — not the Mandarin readings that have been standing in for them.

39 names, titles and lexical items, each character mapped to its Later Han reading from Schuessler's tables. Full coverage: every character resolved, five of them only after Unihan variant matching.

Read this carefully — the columns are not the same thing

  1. The Later Han reading is the Chinese, not the name. It tells you how a Han scribe pronounced the characters he chose. Transcription is lossy: Chinese lacked sounds the source had, dropped final consonants it couldn't render, and broke clusters into extra syllables. 撑犁 was pronounced ḍaŋ lei; the name behind it was something else, close to but not identical with that.
  2. These are mechanical per-character joins. No model, no inference — just Schuessler's reading for each character, in order.
  3. The assessment column is philological judgement, not output. It asks one narrow question: can the Later Han reading be reconciled with what the literature has proposed? It is not a verdict on the proposal.

What “Later Han” means here

Chinese characters have been read aloud differently in every century. The character 突 that spells the first syllable of Türk is tū in Mandarin today; a Tang scribe said something like thwot. Working out what a character sounded like at a particular date is its own scholarly discipline, and the results are called reconstructions — not recordings, but inferences from rhyme dictionaries, foreign transcriptions, dialect evidence and internal patterning.

The reconstructions here are Axel Schuessler's, for the layer he calls Later Han Chinese — the language of roughly 25–220 CE. That period is the point: it is when the Shiji, Hanshu and Hou Hanshu were being written, so it is the pronunciation the scribes who chose these characters actually had. Published as Minimal Old Chinese and Later Han Chinese (2009), and released by Schuessler himself as machine-readable data in December 2024, which is what made this table possible.

So a cell reading ḍaŋ lei for 撑犁 means: around the year 100, a Chinese speaker pronounced these two characters roughly “dang lei”. Nothing more. The Mandarin reading of the same characters is chēnglí, which is about two thousand years of sound change away — and that gap is exactly why the Mandarin forms have been misleading everyone who read them as if they were the names.

Two other reconstruction systems appear where they are cited: Pulleyblank for Early Middle Chinese (c. 600 CE, the Tang layer) and Níng for Old Mandarin (c. 1400, the Ming layer). Different scholars, different notations, different centuries — worth keeping straight when comparing.

The glossed phrase

One item stands apart. The Hanshu doesn't just transcribe it, it translates it — 撑犁 as 天 "heaven", 孤塗 as 子 "son". A foreign phrase with a contemporaneous Chinese gloss is the closest thing this corpus has to a bilingual line.

撑犁

Glossed 天 "heaven". Reconciles closely with Turkic/Mongolic tengri — ḍaŋ-lei against taŋ-ri. The strongest single datum on the Turkic side of the debate.
孤塗
kuɑ dɑ
Glossed 子 "son". Vovin's Yeniseian *qut is one syllable where the Chinese has two, so kuɑ-dɑ fits a disyllabic *quta better than *qut. The strongest datum on the Yeniseian side.
單于
dźan wɑ
The supreme title. Pulleyblank's Middle Chinese *dan-ɣwa sits behind this; proposals include *darγwa and *tarqan. No consensus.

The two halves of one phrase point to different language families. That is the Xiongnu problem in miniature, and it is why a single verdict on the corpus was never likely.

Three things worth noticing

匈奴 was huoŋ nɑ

Not "Xiongnu". The Mandarin form has moved a long way; the Later Han reading is much closer to Hun. That does not settle the Xiongnu–Hun identification — an ethnonym can resemble another by accident, and the 2026 Paleo-Siberian argument rests on quite different evidence — but the resemblance that reading obscures, this one restores.

冒頓 does not look like Baγatur

mək tuənᶜ has no initial b- and no final -r. The Baγatur reading for the empire's founder is widely repeated and the Later Han evidence does not support it. Something like *Mekdun or *Moktun is what the characters give.

撑犁 is the strongest thing in the corpus

ḍaŋ lei against tengri, with the Hanshu glossing it 天 "heaven". Form and meaning both constrained, from a contemporaneous source. Compare the Yuan-era transcription of the same word, 騰格理 teṅgeri, glossed 天 as well — the same word, the same gloss, twelve hundred years apart.

What this is not

It is not a reconstruction of the names. Recovering the source forms would need a transcription channel run backwards, and the measurements say that is not supportable at Han-era data density: 1.4 observations per character, against the 60 that made the same method work on Yuan Mongolian. Whole-form reconstruction tops out around 8% exact at this density.

What is supportable is narrower. Initial place of articulation carries across the period gap at about 79%, and nasal finals at 99% — so onset-level and nasal-final claims stand. Stop finals do not carry at all: Old Mandarin merged -p, -t and -k into nothing, 0 of 45 preserved.
