Supplementary Material S1

Candidate readings of the Xiongnu record

Thirty-four readings of Chinese-transcribed Xiongnu names and titles as Turkic words, with the historical parallels that suggested each one. Supplied for the record; not offered as results.

Aziz M. Uluğ
Supplement to Reading steppe names: what a transcription channel can and cannot recover from the Chinese record of the Xiongnu
S1 · author_proposals.csv · compiled 2026

S1.1What this file is

Section 9 of the paper sets out what a proposed reading of a Xiongnu name has to survive before it can be called a finding: the sense has to be attested in a source independent of the comparison, the morphology has to be licensed by a suffix with the right distribution, and any objection that can be tested has to be tested. One reading in this project meets that standard. The thirty-three others do not.

They are here anyway. They were produced over the course of the work, they are the readings the author actually finds attractive, and suppressing them would be its own kind of dishonesty — a reader who has seen 稽粥 written out as kei çuk will hear Turkish küçük whether or not this file exists. What can be done is to write them down, date them, label them, and put next to each one the reason it occurred to the author, so that the reasoning is inspectable rather than implied.

Nothing in the paper depends on any row below. The measurements in §§5–8 — the channel accuracies, the period-gap decomposition, the initial-consonant probe, the coda-spelling count — were made without reference to these readings and do not change if every one of them is rejected.

S1.2Why they are not in the paper

Section 6.3 measures exactly the operation that produced this file — taking a reconstructed reading and looking it up in a Turkic dictionary — against a null model built by resampling Later Han syllables into pseudo-names. Across seven lexicons, from modern Turkish to the Codex Cumanicus, between none and three names in thirty-six clear p ≤ 0.05. At a one-in-twenty threshold, thirty-six coin flips give the same.

Doing the matching by hand does not improve on the algorithm. It makes it worse, and in a way that is easy to state: a person cannot help preferring the reading that means something, and that preference is precisely the selection effect the null exists to quantify. The algorithm at least fails honestly. A paper that measured the unreliability of this operation and then presented thirty-four of its outputs as discoveries would be incoherent.

So the two things are kept apart. The paper reports what can be measured. This file records what was imagined while measuring it. Because it is supplementary, it does not carry the paper's evidential standard and should not be read as though it did.

S1.3How the readings were produced

Every row starts from the same two published inputs, neither of them model output. The Chinese form is as the Shiji, Hanshu and Hou Hanshu write it. The Later Han reading is Schuessler's, character by character. The third column simply respells that reading in Turkish orthography, so that a reader who does not read phonetic notation can see what is being claimed; it adds no information.

The fourth column is the author's. It is arrived at by hearing the third column as a Turkic word or compound, usually with a vowel changed, sometimes with a consonant treated as lost, and then looking for a historical parallel that would make such a name plausible for a steppe ruler. The fifth column gives the sense that reading would carry. The note beneath each row gives the parallel. Each entry below is set out in two parts: Phonetics, which walks the Later Han reading to the proposed name character by character and says which of the steps are ordinary correspondences and which are not; and Reason, which gives the word, its attestation where it has one, and the naming parallel that suggested it.

Three habits of reasoning recur, and it is worth naming them because they are the file's main weakness. The first is the epithet argument: later Turkic rulers are commonly named by a common noun or a natural force — Bayezid I Yıldırım "the thunderbolt", Alp Arslan "the lion", Mehmed IV Avcı "the hunter", Süleyman I Kanunî "the lawgiver" — so a reading that yields such a noun is treated as more likely. The second is the career argument: where the Chinese record says what a ruler did, a reading that names the deed is preferred. The third is the compound argument: a long transcription is split into two or three Turkic elements. Each of these makes a reading easier to reach, and none of them makes it more likely to be right. They multiply the search space, which is the opposite of evidence.

S1.4The one exception, which is argued in the paper

The recurring element 若鞮 ńɑk te, which closes the formal names of six later chanyu, is read Inakt, "the faithful ones". It is in the paper as §9 rather than here, because it is supported differently from everything else in this file:

  • the Hou Hanshu glosses 若鞮 as 孝 "filial" — the meaning is recorded in the source, not inferred from a resemblance;
  • inak is attested as fidelis "faithful" in the Codex Cumanicus (Kuun 1880, p. 182), a medieval source with no connection to the Chinese record;
  • the morphology is licensed: -t is an Old Turkic plural used with titles (tegin → tegit, tarqan → tarqat), and the competing -tI reading was ruled out by counting — of 31 Cuman headwords in -tI, almost all are past-tense verbs;
  • the obvious objection to the spelling was tested and failed: if the source ended in -t, why did the scribe use the open character 鞮? Because 30% of stop-final source syllables in the Sanskrit corpus were written with an open character.

One objection stands and is stated in the paper: if the source word was ınakt, the initial ı- is not written, and 若鞮 has no room for it. It appears in the tables below inside the six compounds, so that this file can be read as one list with the standard visible in it.

S1.5Why the decoder's output is not cited below

The channel of §4 has a decoding step that will return a ranked list of readings for any string in the record. None of those rankings is quoted in this file, and the reason is worth stating, because the numbers look more informative than they are.

A character can be reading-anchored, meaning its emission distribution was learned for that character specifically, or it can fall back to a table keyed on the Later Han initial consonant class alone, discarding the vowel and the coda. Of the 39 items in the record, only five are reading-anchored throughout; 34 use backoff on at least one character and nine use it on every character. The effect is that whole groups of characters share one distribution:

軍臣Junchenkun dźinkun cinKünçinbright as the sun
Phonetics軍 kun → kün: initial and coda direct; ü is unwritable and surfaces as u. 臣 dźin → çin: dź is the voiced partner of tś, and voicing is leaky in both directions in every corpus measured, so a source ç is reachable through it; the -n coda is written.
Reasonkün “sun, day”. The Seljuk regnal names Kaykhusraw and Kayqubad carry an association of royal solar radiance through their kay- element.
詹師廬Zhanshilutśam ṣi liɑçam şi liaÇamşılıdazzling, radiant

呴犁湖Xulihu / Goulihuhuo lei gɑhuo lei gaKalıksky, heaven
Phonetics呴 huo → ka, 犁 lei → lı, 湖 gɑ → k: three onsets, each taken from the majority correspondence, with nothing inserted and no coda discarded. The velar class covers h-, k- and g- alike, so the first and third syllables cost nothing.
ReasonKāşgarî glosses kalık “hava, gök, sema” — air, sky, heaven. The word belongs to the same semantic field as 撑犁, the one Xiongnu term the Chinese sources gloss (天 “heaven”), so a ruler bearing it would not be isolated in the record. A second exact fit, kılık “disposition, manner”, is available if the sky reading is thought too convenient.
伊稚斜Yizhixieʔi ḍiᶜ ja’i di yaİdiyamaster
Phonetics伊 ʔi → i: a glottal onset is the standard way to write a vowel-initial syllable. 稚 ḍi → di: retroflex ḍ for d is routine. 斜 ja → ya: this character class emits ya more strongly than any other single-syllable correspondence in the record. All three steps are default.
Reasonidi “lord, master”, attested in Old Turkic. Compare Murad I's epithet Hüdavendigâr, “the sovereign”, a title of the same shape and force.
烏維Wuweiʔɑ wi’a viAbıone who hunts or stalks
Phonetics烏 ʔɑ → a: vowel-initial, direct. 維 wi → bı is the weak step: the Chinese character carries a w-, and while Turkic b- and w- alternate, the transcription reads more naturally as avı than abı. This is one of only five items in the record decoded entirely from character-specific evidence, so the mismatch cannot be attributed to a gap in the data.
Reasonav “hunt”, avcı “hunter”. Avcı names an occupation; abı is proposed as naming a trait. Avcı is also the epithet of Mehmed IV.
且鞮侯Qiedihoutsɑᴮ te gotsa te goÇatıgkaone ready for the clash
Phonetics且 tsɑ → ça: ts- for a source affricate is a minority spelling but a real one, 15% in the Sanskrit corpus and 9% in Ligeti. 鞮 te → tı: direct. 侯 go → gka asks a single Chinese syllable to carry a consonant cluster it structurally cannot, so the reading adds material the transcription does not have.
Reasonçat- “to strike together, to clash”. Compare Selçuk Bey, whose name has been read as “perpetually ready for the fight”.
狐鹿姑Huluguɣuɑ lok kɑğua lok kaKurlugathe encloser, the binder
Phonetics姑 kɑ → ga is well supported by the velar correspondence. The other two syllables are not: 狐 ɣuɑ → kur supplies an -r that Later Han has no coda slot for, and 鹿 lok → lu discards a coda that was written. Adding an unwritable segment and dropping a written one in the same word pulls in opposite directions.
Reason狐鹿姑 captured the Han general Li Guangli and held the confederation together through a hard decade, so an epithet of binding fits the record. Compare the Göktürk ruler Kapgan Kagan, kapgan “the encloser”.
壺衍鞮Huyandigɑ janᴮ tega yan teKayantıavalanche
Phonetics壺 gɑ → ka: given a Later Han voiced g-, the source sound is more often k than g, so the devoicing is normal rather than a liberty. 衍 jan → yan: direct, coda written. 鞮 te → tı: direct. All three steps are default or better — phonetically one of the cleaner readings here.
Reasonkay- “to slide, to slip away”. Parallel in sense to Bayezid I's Yıldırım: a natural force taken as a regnal epithet.
虛閭權渠Xulüquanqukʰɨɑ liɑ gyan gɨɑkıa lia gyan gıaKalkanshield
Phonetics虛 kʰɨɑ → ka, 閭 liɑ → l, 權 gyan → kan: the first three characters match exactly, including the written -n coda, at zero minority steps. Only 渠 gɨɑ is left unaccounted for, and it may carry a separate title element.
Reasonkalkan “shield” is in Kāşgarî and in the Codex Cumanicus as Kalkani, “scutum ejus”. A weapon or armour name is an ordinary steppe formation, and it needs no compound to be read.
握衍朐鞮Woyanqudiʔɔk janᴮ guo te’ok yan guo teOkyan Kutuğtıblessed sovereign of the arrow flank
Phonetics握 ʔɔk → ok is exact, with the -k coda written, and 衍 jan → yan and 鞮 te → tı are direct. The weak point is 朐 guo → kutuğ, which expands one Chinese syllable into two Turkic ones; nothing in the corpora licenses that expansion.
Reasonok “arrow” doubles as a division of the confederation in later Turkic usage — the On Ok, “ten arrows”, of the Western Turks — which is what makes a territorial reading available. kut is “heavenly favour, fortune”.
呼韓邪Huhanyehɑ gɑn jaha gan yaKaganyakagan of the flank
Phonetics呼 hɑ → ka: an h/k substitution, which the Mongolian corpus supports strongly (a source k or q was written with an h- character 27% of the time). 韓 gɑn → gan: exact, coda written. 邪 ja → ya: the strongest single correspondence in the record. The written sequence ha gan ya becomes ka-gan-ya on one change.
Reason呼韓邪 commanded the left flank before becoming ruler, so the Chinese record itself supplies the office that the reading names.
郅支Zhizhitśit tśeçit çeÇitçiborder-maker, one who carves the boundary
Phonetics郅 tśit → çit: tś- is the default affricate vehicle and the -t coda is written. 支 tśe → çi: default. Both syllables use the majority correspondence and neither requires an inserted or deleted segment.
Reasonçit / çiz-, of marking or carving a boundary. 郅支 broke away westward and set up a rival seat, so a name of division fits the career — though it is equally possible that the career suggested the name.

骨都侯guduhoukuət tɑ gokuıt ta goKutakthe blessed one
Phonetics骨 kuət → kut: default velar onset with the -t coda written — the tightest single syllable in this file. 都 tɑ then begins with the same dental, and step 22 measures what that means: at a junction where a written coda is followed by the same-place onset, the source word carries that consonant once rather than twice in 121 of 140 cases (86%). Reasonkut is “kut, uğur, devlet, baht, talih, saadet” in Kāşgarî — heavenly favour, fortune, sovereign grace — and Clauson records the verb kutad- “to be blessed”, the stem behind the Kutadgu Bilig. Kutak itself is not attested: it would be a deverbal or diminutive formation in -(A)k on that stem, which is a live suffix but not one witnessed on this word. Compare Murad I's Hüdavendigâr and Qutalmış, ancestor of the Seljuks of Rum. Note also that kut is the Turkic candidate for 孤塗, where the Chinese gloss 子 “son” fits the Yeniseian proposal better and not this one.
且渠juqutsɑᴮ gɨɑtsa gıaÇakathe striker; from çak- ‘to strike fire, to strike a blow’
Phonetics且 tsɑᴮ → ça: ts- for a source affricate is a minority spelling but an attested one — 15% in the Sanskrit corpus, 9% in Ligeti — against a tś- character as the default at 37%. 渠 gɨɑ → ka: a Later Han voiced g- stands for a foreign k more often than for a foreign g. Both steps are permitted by the measured correspondences.
Reasonçak- “to strike fire from flint, to sting, to slander”, which Clauson describes as onomatopoeic in character and of violent action, physical or mental. No çaka headword exists in Kāşgarî, the Codex or Clauson — only the verb and its derivatives çakmak, çakır, çakılmak — so Çaka would have to be the -A converb of that stem, and a bare converb serving as an office title is not a demonstrable pattern. The 11th-century emir Çaka Bey of Smyrna (Byzantine Τζαχᾶς, fl. 1081–93) shows that çak- could yield a personal name, but that is a personal name set against a title. The Chinese sources do not record what the office of 且渠 did, so no sense can be tested against a gloss.
徑路jinglukeŋᶜ lɑᶜkeng laKıngraka large cleaver-like knife
Phonetics徑 keŋᶜ → kıng: default velar onset with the -ŋ coda written. 路 lɑᶜ → ra: a Chinese l- for a source r-, which step 15 shows is the ordinary direction — a source r- was written l- in 12 of 14 attestations. The final -k of the Turkic word is not written, and step 20 licenses that: 30% of stop-final source syllables were spelled with an open character.
ReasonThis reading is not the author's. The comparison of 徑路 with Turkic kıŋrak is already in the literature, where it is described as speculative; it entered this project during the corpus build, recorded as cited and unverified. What is new here is that it can be tested, and it survives. Kāşgarî glosses kıñrak as “et ve hamur kesilen satıra benzer büyük bıçak” — a large knife resembling a cleaver — and the Chinese sources independently describe 徑路 as a Xiongnu blade used in oath-taking (the 徑路刀). Two sources with no contact agree about what the object is, which is a stronger kind of agreement than a sound match. A word of identical shape, kañrak “palate”, fits the transcription equally well and is excluded only by the referent. §9.1 of the paper sets this out; the original publication of the comparison still has to be traced.

S1.11How to disagree

The repository includes src/lookup_lexicons.py, which searches Kāşgarî's Dīwān, the Codex Cumanicus and any EPUB lexicon placed beside them, folding the spelling conventions that separate Turcological from Turkish orthography (q ≡ k, ñ ≡ ng, ş ≡ s, ı ≡ i). Type any proposed form into it and see what the medieval sources actually have. That is how the Codex attestation for inak was found, and how the -tI count that ruled out the competing suffix was made.

A reader who wants to dispose of a row below should find that easier than the author did. The useful form of disagreement is not "this is unlikely" — most of them are — but "this word is not attested before date X", or "this suffix does not attach to that stem", or "the Chinese character cannot carry that coda". Those are the objections that would move something out of this file, in either direction.

S1.12Status

Every reading in Tables S2–S6 carries the same label in the machine-readable file: author's proposal; not supported by any measurement in the paper. That label is the honest summary of all thirty-three, and it is not weakened by the fact that some of them are more attractive than others. The one reading with a different status, 若鞮 → Inakt, is argued in §9 of the paper and summarised in §S1.4 above.
