Reading Xiongnu names: a transcription channel for Chinese records of steppe languages

Two new corpora, a statistical model of how Chinese scribes wrote foreign names, and measured limits on what the surviving evidence can support.

Aziz M. Uluğ, PhD  ·  Cortechs.ai  ·  26 August 2026
Draft for discussion. Written for readers outside computational linguistics; Appendix A explains every technical term; Appendix B collects the author's own speculative readings, which are not results.

Abstract

Chinese histories record hundreds of Inner Asian names — Xiongnu rulers, Turkic titles, tribal designations — in Chinese characters chosen for their sound. Because Chinese pronunciation has changed enormously in two thousand years, the modern Mandarin readings of those characters bear little relation to what the scribes heard. This work builds a statistical model of the transcription process itself, trains it on cases where both sides are known, and measures what it can and cannot recover.

Four things came out. First, three machine-readable training sets. Two are corpora that did not previously exist: 9,329 Chinese–Mongolian transcription pairs from the Secret History of the Mongols, and 594 Chinese–Uyghur pairs from Ligeti's edition of a Ming glossary. The third is 1,017 verified Chinese–Sanskrit pairs, separated out of a 27,110-entry Buddhist dictionary that mixes phonetic spellings with translations. Second, two medieval Turkic dictionaries as machine-readable lexicons with glosses, for which no downloadable dataset could be found: 6,820 headwords with glosses from the Türk Dil Kurumu index to Kāşgarî's Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk (1072–77), and 2,110 Cuman headwords with Latin glosses from Kuun's 1880 edition of the Codex Cumanicus. Third, a working model — trained in-domain it reconstructs 31% of held-out Turkic words exactly and 84% within two sounds. Fourth, and most useful, two negative results with measured ceilings: determining which language a name belongs to is not supportable on available reference data, and full pronunciation reconstruction of the Xiongnu names is not supportable at the density of evidence that survives — 1.4 observations per character, against the 60 that made the same method work on Mongolian.

What is supportable is narrower and precise. Initial consonants carry across the 1,200-year gap between the Chinese sources at about 79% fidelity, and nasal endings at 99%; stop endings do not carry at all. The paper closes by arguing that Göktürk-era material, where the source language is not in dispute, is a far better target than the Xiongnu and would supply the training data this project lacked.

1The problem

Chinese has no alphabet. To write a foreign name, a Chinese scribe picks characters that sound approximately right in his own speech. The Han historians did this constantly: the Shiji, Hanshu and Hou Hanshu are full of Xiongnu rulers, titles and tribal names spelled out this way.

The difficulty is that Chinese pronunciation has moved a very long way since. Take 突厥, the name of the Türk people. Today a Mandarin speaker reads it Tūjué, which sounds nothing like Türk. But in the Tang dynasty, when it was written, those characters were pronounced something like *dwət-kuat — and that does sound like Türk. The final consonants that carry the whole resemblance, the t and the k, disappeared from Chinese a thousand years ago.

So the modern readings are not merely imprecise. They are systematically misleading, and anyone who reasons from them is reasoning from a corrupted signal. The first requirement of any serious attempt is to work with reconstructed period pronunciations1 rather than modern ones.

Even with the right period readings, a second problem remains. The Chinese spelling tells you what the scribe said, not what the foreigner said. The scribe had a limited inventory of syllables, could not pronounce sounds his language lacked, and dropped or added material to fit. Recovering the original from the transcription is an inverse problem: you observe a degraded signal and want the source.

2What we built: the channel

The core object is what we call a transcription channel. It is a statistical model of the scribe: given a sound in a foreign name, which Chinese characters was he likely to write?2

An analogy for readers from imaging or signal processing. The channel is a forward model — a point spread function. The "object" is the spoken foreign name; the "acquisition system" is a scribe with a restricted phoneme inventory who blurs it, truncates its edges and merges detail he cannot resolve. Reconstruction is deconvolution, and every result below is a statement about how much of the object survives the transfer function.

The channel is learned, not designed. Feed it examples where both sides are known — a foreign word and the Chinese characters used to write it — and it works out the correspondences on its own, including which characters conventionally marked sounds Chinese did not have.3

One design choice mattered more than any other. The obvious approach keys the model on the character. That fails here, because there are thousands of characters and only a handful of examples of each. Keying it instead on the character's reconstructed reading pools evidence across every character pronounced the same way, and lifted performance by 16 points at no extra data.4

3Three corpora and two lexicons

A learned model needs examples, and for this problem almost none existed in machine-readable form. Three transcription corpora were built — two extracted from sources that had never been digitised, and one separated out of a dictionary that mixed usable examples with unusable ones. Two further datasets of a different kind, medieval Turkic dictionaries rather than transcription pairs, were built later for the tests in §5; they are described at the end of this section because they are outputs of the work in the same sense that the corpora are.

Corpus 1 — Secret History of the Mongols

The Secret History is a thirteenth-century Mongolian chronicle that survives in a remarkable form: the entire Mongolian text written out in Chinese characters, with a romanised reading printed alongside. It is a continuous running transcription, not a dictionary — which matters, because dictionaries mix phonetic spellings with translated ones.

9,329 unique pairs, 37,250 character tokens over 535 distinct characters. Extracted from an aligned digital edition. As a check on the extraction, 93.4% of pairs agree within ±1 between the number of Chinese characters and the number of syllables in the Mongolian — the expected signature of a genuine syllable-by-syllable transcription.

Corpus 2 — Ligeti's Sino-Uyghur vocabulary

A Ming-dynasty Chinese–Uyghur glossary, edited and published by Louis Ligeti in 1966 and 1969. Ligeti prints no Chinese characters; he gives the Chinese transcription in a French romanisation, which turned out to suit the reading-keyed model perfectly.

594 unique pairs, 217 distinct Chinese syllables. Small, but it is the only Turkic-language transcription corpus of any size in machine-readable form.

Why the coda markers matter. Ming Chinese had lost most final consonants, so scribes marked foreign endings with an extra character instead: 兒 for a final -r, 思 for a final -s. These are the two most frequent syllables in the corpus, at 118 and 61 occurrences. The information the language had lost was re-encoded in the spelling system — which is why the model works despite the loss.

Corpus 3 — verified Buddhist Sanskrit transcriptions

The largest body of Chinese transcription anywhere is Buddhist: for centuries Chinese translators wrote out Sanskrit terms phonetically, and we know the Sanskrit exactly. A digitised dictionary of this material already exists — the Fo Guang Shan / NTI dictionary, 27,110 entries.

It cannot be used as it stands, and the reason is the interesting part. Chinese rendered foreign words two entirely different ways, and the dictionary mixes them:

  • 音譯 — phonetic. 釋迦牟尼 for śākyamuni: the characters were chosen for sound, and mean nothing relevant.
  • 意譯 — semantic. 大乘 for mahāyāna: literally "great vehicle", a translation of the meaning with no relation to the sound.

Only the first kind is usable. Training a sound model on translations would teach it correspondences that do not exist. Separating them turned out to be genuinely hard, and two approaches failed before one worked:

  • Character lists — collect the characters known to be used phonetically, then accept any word made of them. This swallowed common characters like 佛, 天, 王, after which every entry passed and translations such as 福慧 = puṇya slipped into the training set.
  • Character exclusivity — score each character by what fraction of its uses are phonetic. This punished precisely the characters that do double duty, which is most of them, and collapsed the set to 67 usable pairs.
  • Phonetic verification — what worked. Line each character up with its Sanskrit syllable and check the reconstructed Later Han reading against it. 摩 was *mɑ and its syllable is ma ✓. In 福慧, 慧 was *ɦuei against ṇya ✗ — so the word is rejected.

1,017 verified pairs, from 27,110 candidates.

This corpus is a different kind of output from the other two — it was separated out of an existing digital source rather than extracted from an undigitised one. Its value is that it is verified: every pair has been checked for phonetic plausibility, and the 7,480 rejected entries are retained separately so the decision can be audited.

Lexicons 1 and 2 — the Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk and the Codex Cumanicus

The three corpora above are all of the same shape: a foreign word paired with the Chinese characters used to write it. The tests in §5 needed something different — dictionaries of Turkic itself, from near the period, against which a reading could be looked up. No downloadable dataset of either was found, so both were extracted. Uppsala's Middle Turkic Database is building digital editions of Middle Turkic sources under CC BY 4.0 and may in time supersede this, but at the time of writing its corpus is Middle Karaim and covers neither of these works.

Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk (1072–77) is the earliest dictionary of any Turkic language, compiled in Baghdad by Kāşgarlı Mahmud. The Türk Dil Kurumu index volume has a machine-readable text layer; parsing it yields 6,820 headwords, each with Kāşgarî's Turkish gloss and his volume-and-page references, as dlt_lexicon.csv.11

The Codex Cumanicus (c. 1300) records Kipchak Turkic, collected by Italian merchants and German missionaries — outsiders writing down a language they did not speak, which is the same position the Chinese scribes were in. Géza Kuun's 1880 edition prints a Cumano-Latin vocabulary at pages 395–455 of the scan; parsing that section yields 2,110 headwords with their Latin glosses and codex page references, as cuman_lexicon.csv. The Persian and German vocabularies that follow were deliberately skipped: the Persian one in particular would have injected Iranian words into a Turkic lexicon and quietly inflated every match rate computed from it.12

They differ in what can be done with them. Kuun's edition is from 1880 and is public domain, so cuman_lexicon.csv can be released with this work — it is the only derived dataset here with no restriction on it at all. The Kāşgarî index is a modern Türk Dil Kurumu edition, so dlt_lexicon.csv inherits whatever applies to that edition; it is documented and reproducible from the source, but not redistributed here. The extraction script is released in both cases, which is what makes the numbers checkable either way.

Both are byproducts in origin and neither was the goal, but a 6,820-entry Kāşgarî and a 2,110-entry Cuman lexicon in CSV are useful well beyond this paper — for anyone who wants to search medieval Turkic vocabulary by form rather than by page.

5What the evidence cannot support

Two claims commonly made from this kind of material turned out not to hold up, and establishing that carefully is arguably the most useful output of the work.

The objection is not new, and not the author's

A reader from historical linguistics will recognise everything in this section, and it is worth saying plainly that none of it is this paper's invention. Gerhard Doerfer made the argument in 1973, in Lautgesetz und Zufall — "Sound Law and Chance" — a study of what he called omnicomparatism, the practice of relating languages across great distances on the strength of resemblances.5 Chance produces such resemblances in quantity, was Doerfer's case, and comparativists have a repertoire of devices — he called them tricks — for making weak matches look strong. He included deliberately absurd comparisons of his own to show how easily the devices work.

A review in Anthropos the following year pressed the point into a question this project can answer with numbers.13 Sound laws, the reviewer observed, are not deterministic but probabilistic, and the literature never says how probabilistic they are permitted to be. Is it a sound law if 49% of cases show the same correspondence and 51% show something else? Where does regularity stop? The reviewer proposed three classes: deterministic laws, which hold without exception; strong probabilistic laws, where one correspondence occurs in more than half of cases; and weak probabilistic laws, where one correspondence is merely the most common.

Table 5 answers that question for this material — with confidence intervals, and with a correction to the reviewer's own threshold that only counting could have revealed. The answer is set out where the measurements are, in §6.

The reviewer also pointed to where the mathematics already lay: the matching problem of Montmort (1708), which asks how often two shuffled sequences agree by accident. The permutation nulls used throughout this paper are that problem solved by simulation instead of by formula. Nothing here is methodologically new. What is new is only that the numbers are computed on this material rather than asserted — which is what Doerfer was asking for.

Determining which language a name belongs to

The obvious application is settling whether the Xiongnu ruling house spoke Turkic, Yeniseian, Mongolic or something else. We built the comparison properly — score how well each candidate language explains the transcription, and compare8.

It failed its own control tests. Given Ming Uyghur transcriptions whose language is not in dispute, the method identified them as Turkic 8.6% of the time against a 16.7% chance baseline — worse than guessing. A follow-up experiment handed the language models the true source word, bypassing the Chinese entirely, to establish an upper bound. The best result anywhere across 24 configurations was 69% against a 50% baseline, and two configurations came out below chance, indicating systematic bias rather than mere weakness.

The cause is not fixable by better modelling. Turkic, Mongolic and Tungusic are phonotactically near-identical — they permit the same sound patterns — and roughly half the available reference vocabulary is single-syllable words, where the best distinguishing feature (vowel harmony) is undefined. This is a fact about the languages.

Reconstructing the Xiongnu names in full

At 1.4 observations per character, whole-word reconstruction tops out around 8% exact. A near-miss during the work is worth recording: an earlier version passed a quality gate based on same-domain accuracy and printed a confident table of Xiongnu reconstructions. Checked against out-of-domain accuracy — the figure the Xiongnu material actually inherits — the true reliability was 3.1%. The output looked entirely plausible. It was noise.

Matching the readings to modern Turkic words

There is an obvious next step, and it is the one every reader wants to take. Once 稽粥 is written out as kei çuk, it is hard not to hear Turkish küçük, "small, younger" — a plausible enough thing to call a son. The method is old and simple: look the reading up in a modern dictionary and see what it hits.

The trouble is that it always works. Modern Turkish has some twenty thousand words in common use; a target with three consonants has hundreds of near neighbours in it. So the question is never "is there a match" — there is always a match — but "is there more of a match than a string that cannot mean anything would get?" The same permutation logic used everywhere else here measures that: build pseudo-names by resampling Later Han syllables from these same 39 names, so they have the right shape and the right sound inventory and no meaning at all, then run them through the identical search.10

Two objections arrive immediately, and both are testable rather than arguable. First: most of the matches are loanwords — kauçuk is French caoutchouc, and a second-century-BCE steppe name cannot echo a word Turkish borrowed in the nineteenth century; so filter to native Turkic vocabulary. Second: search Turkish and Kazakh together, since two dictionaries should beat one. Six lexicons were built and each was given its own null.

The loanword objection is right, and the filter does what it should: dropping words that begin f-, l-, r-, z-, j-, v-, h-, contain f or j, end in a voiced stop, or break vowel harmony removes domino, makedon, kantin and linyit, and the noise floor rises accordingly (0.156 → 0.200). It is a phonotactic proxy, not an etymological dictionary, and it is porous — kauçuk and tango both survive it, being back-harmonic and cluster-free. But cleaning the lexicon did not turn up any name that was not already there.

The combining objection has a sharper answer, and it is the reverse of the intuition. Pooling the two dictionaries makes the result worse. The union finds more matches for the real names, as expected — but it finds more for the meaningless strings too, and slightly faster: the noise floor falls from 0.156 to 0.117 and the number of names clearing chance does not move. A larger haystack does not help you find a needle; it just contains more hay. What does help is the opposite operation. Keeping only words present in both languages cuts the lexicon to 1,497 entries and lifts the noise floor to 0.329, because a word has to survive two independent borrowing histories to get in.

Except that this cure has its own disease. The intersection is over identical spellings, and cognates diverge: Turkish tanrı and Kazakh täñir are the same word and do not match as strings, so both fall out. The intersection keeps domino and kauçuk — borrowed into both languages recently and spelled alike — while discarding the deep Turkic inheritance it was built to isolate. It enriches for shared modern loans.

Which brings the two known answers back, now across all six lexicons:

  • 撑犁 is the strongest datum in the corpus — the Hanshu glosses it 天 "heaven", so tengri, whose modern Turkish descendant is Tanrı, is as close to a known answer as this material offers. In every lexicon that contains tanrı, the search returns dengeli, "balanced", at 0.03 and puts tanrı at 0.62 — twenty times worse. In the two intersection lexicons tanrı is absent altogether.
  • 頭曼 has the most widely accepted etymology of any Xiongnu name, tuman / tümen. Only in the smallest, most heavily filtered lexicon does tümen appear in the top three at all — third, at 0.18, behind duman "smoke" at 0.04 and ataman at 0.16.

And the example that started this: kei çuk gives küçük at 0.083 — but it gives kaçak "fugitive" and kaçık "crazy" at exactly the same 0.083, and kauçuk at 0.056, which is better. The search cannot prefer the meaningful one, because it does not know what any of them mean.

So the honest summary is that the dictionary can be cleaned, and cleaning it helps — the noise floor more than doubles between the raw union and the filtered intersection. It still is not enough. In no lexicon does either known answer come first, and in the lexicon where the noise floor is highest, the correct answer has been filtered out along with the noise. A procedure that cannot recover the answers we already have cannot be trusted with the ones we do not.

The same search against medieval dictionaries

Both objections above share a deeper one that neither addresses: modern Turkish is the wrong lexicon in the first place. It is two thousand years downstream of the Xiongnu, and every word in it has had that long to be replaced, borrowed or shifted. The obvious correction is to search a dictionary from nearer the period.

Kāşgarî's Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk, compiled in Baghdad between 1072 and 1077, is the earliest dictionary of any Turkic language.11 It is still a thousand years after the Xiongnu, but it is a thousand years closer, it contains no European loans whatever, and it is small — which, by the logic of the table above, raises the noise floor for free. It also carries glosses, so a candidate arrives with its meaning attached rather than being interpreted afterwards. The Turkish Language Society index volume has a machine-readable text layer; 6,820 headwords were extracted from it, of which 6,337 are single words.11

The noise floor rose to 0.200, as predicted. More to the point, the two known answers came back — both are in Kāşgarî, and both now appear.

A second medieval lexicon sharpens the picture further. The Codex Cumanicus, compiled around 1300, records Kipchak Turkic — a different branch from Kāşgarî's Karakhanid — and was compiled by Italian merchants and German missionaries, that is, by outsiders writing down a language they did not speak, which is precisely the situation the Chinese scribes were in. Géza Kuun's 1880 edition prints a Cumano-Latin vocabulary; 2,110 entries were extracted from it, giving 2,067 search forms.12 Being smaller again, it raises the noise floor again, to 0.242 — the highest of the six lexicons tested.

The second row repays attention, because it cuts against the paper's own table. The accepted etymology of 頭曼 is tümen, "ten thousand", and Table 8 marks it consistent. But tümen is absent from the Cuman vocabulary altogether, and in every lexicon that has both, the form the search actually prefers belongs to a different lexeme: modern Turkish duman, Kāşgarî's tuman "duman, sis", Cuman touman "nebula" — smoke, fog, mist, one word across three lexicons and a thousand years. The difference between tuman and tümen is a vowel, and §6 puts vowel recovery at 50%, so this does not settle anything. It is worth recording only because the alternative is never mentioned in the literature, and because a name meaning "mist" or "dust-cloud" is no less plausible for a steppe ruler than one meaning "ten thousand".

Two corrections were needed to get this far, and both are worth recording because both were silently wrong before. Later Han Chinese had no r-; step 15's correspondence table shows a source r- written with a Chinese l- character in 12 of 14 attestations across the three corpora. An l facing an r is therefore not a mismatch but the expected spelling, and pricing it by the generic rule was penalising the correct answer. Separately, a bug in the segmenter was discarding the velar nasal ŋ whenever it appeared as a single character rather than as ng — which removed tengri from consideration entirely. Both fixes came from looking at why a known answer was missing, which is the only reliable way to find this class of error.

What has and has not changed. Neither known answer ranks first. 撑犁 still goes to dangal "chaff-cutter" on a perfect consonant match, and 頭曼 to tuman "smoke" — and three of thirty-six names clearing p ≤ 0.05 is still about what thirty-six coin flips give. So §5 stands: this does not reconstruct the Xiongnu names. What changed is narrower and still worth having. Against a period-appropriate lexicon the correct answers are visible in the top four instead of absent, which means the failure in the modern run was partly the lexicon's fault and not only the method's. That is a difference between "the signal is not there" and "we were looking in the wrong place", and it is worth knowing which.

"Survives" is leave-one-out majority prediction of the earlier reading from the later one; intervals are Wilson score intervals; "chance baseline" is the unconditional majority class for that part — the score you would get by always guessing the commonest value, knowing nothing about the character in front of you. Lift is column two minus column four: how many percentage points knowing the later reading actually buys you. That is the honest test of whether a correspondence carries information, and it is not the same test as "is it above one half", as the last column explains. The classes in the last column are those proposed in the 1974 Anthropos review of Doerfer.13,5 Do not read an aggregate coda figure: blending nasals and stops gives a flattering 87% that means nothing, because the sample is 72% nasal-ending characters.

This table is an answer to Doerfer's question. §5 quoted the challenge put in 1974 by the unsigned reviewer of Doerfer's book in the journal Anthropos13 — a critic writing in Doerfer's defence, pressing his argument further than Doerfer had: sound laws are probabilistic, nobody says how probabilistic they may be, so is it a law if 49% of cases show one correspondence and 51% show another? That reviewer's proposed classes — deterministic, strong probabilistic above one half, weak probabilistic below it — could not be applied then, because nobody had the numbers. The last column applies them.

Three of the four components classify cleanly. Nasal endings, at 117 of 118, are deterministic for any practical purpose. Initial consonants, at 79.3% with the interval nowhere near one half, are a strong probabilistic law. Stop endings are not a law in any sense: not one of 45 survived, and the interval tops out at 7.9%.

The fourth is the interesting one, and it exposes a flaw in the reviewer's own rule. Vowels land at 50.0% — 82 of 164, on the boundary to the decimal — and the confidence interval, 42.4 to 57.6, straddles it. By his criterion the vowel cannot be classified at all, and no larger sample would fix that, because the estimate is not uncertain; it is genuinely at one half.

But one half is the right threshold only when there are two outcomes to choose between. A vowel is a choice among many, and the honest comparison is not against 50% but against what you would get knowing nothing — the majority class, here 15.2%. Against that, vowels carry +34.8 points of real information. They are a weak probabilistic law in the reviewer's third sense, not a coin flip. Stop endings, by the same measure, fall below their baseline: knowing the Ming reading of a stop-final character makes you worse off than guessing, because Old Mandarin merged -p, -t and -k into nothing and the merger is not recoverable.

So the fifty-year-old question has a shape the fifty-year-old debate could not see: the threshold is not a constant. It is the baseline for the component in question, and it moves with how many outcomes that component has. That correction costs nothing to state and could not have been stated without counting.

So the boundary is precise. Claims about the initial consonant of a Xiongnu syllable are supportable. Claims about a nasal ending are supportable. Claims requiring a specific stop ending are not supportable at all — the evidence for which stop it was does not survive.

Separately, the Later Han readings of the characters themselves are simply available from published reconstructions, and are not in dispute. All 39 Xiongnu names, titles and lexical items are set out in the two tables at the end of this section.9 Three observations from it are worth flagging:

  • 匈奴, the ethnonym, was huoŋ nɑ — much closer to Hun than the modern Xiongnu suggests. This does not settle the Xiongnu–Hun question, but the resemblance the modern reading hides, the period reading restores.
  • 冒頓, the empire's founder, was mək tuənᶜ. The widely repeated reading Baγatur requires an initial b- and a final -r, neither of which is there. The first half of that is testable, and is tested immediately below.
  • 撑犁 was ḍaŋ lei, and the Hanshu glosses it 天 "heaven" — so form and meaning are both fixed by a contemporaneous source. Against Turkic tengri this is the strongest single datum in the corpus. It is also, on closer inspection, the most treacherous: see immediately below.

Why 撑犁 proves less than it appears to. The match between ḍaŋ lei and tengri is the single most quoted piece of evidence for a Turkic Xiongnu, and this paper has leaned on it too. The difficulty is not with the match, which is good, but with what follows from it.

The word is not confined to Turkic. Mongolic has tenger "sky"; Turkic has tengri, surviving into modern Turkish as Tanrı "God"; and Georg has argued that the Turko-Mongolic word is itself built on Proto-Yeniseian *tǝŋgVr- "high" with a Turkic suffix, making it a borrowing into Turkic rather than an inheritance within it.8,24 Georg's derivation is one argument among several and need not be accepted. What matters here holds either way: a word attested across Turkic, Mongolic and — on Georg's account — Yeniseian cannot discriminate between them.

So 撑犁 establishes something real and something narrower than is usually claimed. It shows that the Xiongnu used, for "heaven", a word that is also found in Turkic and Mongolic — which is a genuine link to the later steppe world, and the only Xiongnu word whose form and meaning are both fixed by a contemporaneous source. It does not show which of those languages they spoke. That is the "names travel" problem of §8 arriving in the one place the evidence looked strongest, and it is the reason §5's conclusion holds even here.

A test the corpora can run: is m- ever a b-?

The middle observation is worth pressing, because it is a claim the corpora can settle rather than assert. Reading 冒頓 as Baγatur requires the m- to be standing in for a b-. Proto-Turkic has no initial *m- at all: the m- words in Old Turkic are either loans, or come from *b- assimilating to a nasal that immediately follows the vowel — *bän → men, *buŋ → muŋ, *bin- → min-.4,7 So on the Turkic reading the substitution is not optional; it is load-bearing.

What it does is put a number on a substitution that is usually assumed silently: 3 in 838. Together with the absent -r, that gives two independent lines of evidence against the most-repeated etymology in the field — one from the initial, one from the ending. Neither depends on the channel model; both are counts over corpora that anyone can recount.

7Beyond the Xiongnu: a better target

The Xiongnu are the hardest case available, for a reason that has nothing to do with method: nobody knows what language they spoke, so there is no gold standard to train or test against. Every other Inner Asian group recorded in Chinese is easier.

The Göktürk material (552–744 CE) is a far better target, and I would argue it is where this work should go next. Its language is not in dispute — the Orkhon inscriptions are written in Old Turkic, and there is a full lexicon and grammar for it. Many Göktürk names appear both in the runiform inscriptions and, in Chinese transcription, in the Tang histories.

Those double attestations are exactly the training data this project lacked. Securely identified examples include 突厥 Türk, 阿史那 Ashina (the ruling clan), 可汗 qaγan, 葉護 yabghu, 特勤 tigin, 設 šad, 達干 tarqan, 闕特勤 Kül Tigin, 毗伽 Bilge, and 暾欲谷 Tonyuquq. A systematic sweep of the Zhoushu, Suishu and the two Tang histories would plausibly yield a few hundred pairs.

Two further advantages compound. The Göktürk material is Tang-era, so the relevant Chinese layer is Early Middle Chinese — the period for which Pulleyblank's reconstructions exist. And by the measurements in §6, the step from Tang back to Later Han preserves initial consonants at 93.9%, against 79.3% from Ming. A Tang-trained channel is therefore much better positioned to reach the Xiongnu material than the Ming-trained one used here.

Which groups this can and cannot reach

The obvious next question is how widely this applies. Chinese records name a great many steppe peoples, and they fall into three quite different categories.

Tractable. Groups recorded in the Tang histories whose language is known or strongly agreed: 鐵勒 Tiele, 回紇 Uyghur, 薛延陀 Sir-Tarduš, 葛邏祿 Qarluq, 突騎施 Türgesh, 黠戛斯 Kyrgyz, 沙陀 Shatuo, alongside the Göktürk material. These have Chinese transcriptions, a securely identified source language, and a reconstruction layer with published readings. They are the training data.

Same problem as the Xiongnu. Groups in the earlier records whose language is disputed — 丁零 Dingling, 柔然 Rouran (sometimes identified with the Avars, contestedly), 烏桓 Wuhuan, 鮮卑 Xianbei. Their names can be read — 柔然 was ńu ńan, 烏桓 was ʔɑ ɣuɑn — but reading is not the same as reconstructing, and with the source language unknown there is no gold standard to check against. Everything in §5 applies to them unchanged.

One of these is a methodological gift. 丁零 Dingling (Han sources) and 鐵勒 Tiele (Tang sources) are conventionally taken to be the same people. Their Later Han readings are teŋ leŋ and tʰet lək — the same name transcribed twice, six centuries apart, with two different character choices. Pairs like this are exactly what is needed to calibrate the period bridge on real data rather than on reconstruction tables. The same is true of 撑犁 and Yuan-era 騰格理, both glossed "heaven" and both standing for tengri.

Out of reach entirely. The Pechenegs, Cumans and Khazars are barely present in Chinese sources at all — they are documented in Byzantine Greek, Arabic and Persian. No Chinese transcription means nothing for a Chinese channel to work on. The method transfers, but the model does not: recovering Pecheneg names would mean building a Greek or Arabic transcription channel from scratch, trained on Greek or Arabic renderings of names we independently know. That is the same project in a different script, and it would need its own corpora.

I put all of this forward as a research direction rather than a finding. I have not verified these identifications against the specialist literature, some are certainly contested, and the Dingling–Tiele equation in particular is an inherited assumption I am in no position to assess. Establishing which pairs are secure enough to serve as gold data is exactly the judgement a Turkologist would supply, and it is the first thing such a collaborator should be asked.

8Limitations

  • No independent philological review. The computational method and its evaluation are the author's to defend. The philology is not the author's work and has not been checked for this paper: the Later Han readings are Schuessler's, the etymologies assessed in Tables 8 and 9 are drawn from the published literature, and the identifications in Table 10 are the established consensus. A specialist reading of those materials would strengthen the paper and might revise the fit judgements in Tables 8 and 9, which are the author's readings of that literature rather than settled results.
  • Corpus validation is internal. The extraction rules are documented and internally consistent (93.4% length agreement for Mongolian), but no external reviewer has checked a sample against the sources.
  • The Turkic corpus is Ming-era, roughly 1,200 years later than the Xiongnu material. §6 measures what that costs.
  • Reconstructions are inferences. Schuessler, Pulleyblank and Níng are three different scholars with three notations; some apparent disagreement is notational rather than substantive.
  • Names travel. Even a perfect reconstruction would not settle ethnicity — Turkic tribes bore Iranian names, Mongol rulers used Turkic titles.
  • Licensing of the Ligeti corpus is unresolved and may restrict its redistribution.

9Conclusion

The method works where the evidence is dense enough, and the density required is now known. The Xiongnu material falls an order of magnitude short of it, and no amount of modelling closes that gap — which is itself worth saying clearly in a field where confident reconstructions from this evidence are routine and their uncertainty is rarely stated.

What the work leaves behind is two reusable corpora, a documented and reproducible method, and measured ceilings on three separate claims. The productive next step is not more computation on the Xiongnu but a Göktürk-era corpus, where the source language is known and the period gap is half as wide.

AAppendix: the techniques, explained

Supplementary to the main text. Each entry corresponds to a numbered marker above.

  1. Reconstructed pronunciation. Nobody recorded Han-dynasty Chinese. Its sound is inferred from rhyme dictionaries, from how Chinese wrote foreign words whose pronunciation we know, from modern dialects and from borrowings into Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese. The results are called reconstructions and are written with an asterisk or in phonetic notation. This work uses Axel Schuessler's for Later Han (c. 25–220 CE), Edwin Pulleyblank's for Early Middle Chinese (c. 600) and Níng's for Old Mandarin (c. 1400).
  2. Channel model. A probability table: for each source sound, how often each Chinese spelling was used. Formally it estimates P(Chinese spelling | source sound). The term comes from information theory, where a "channel" is anything that carries a signal and distorts it. Running it backwards — asking which source sound best explains an observed spelling — is the reconstruction step, and is why a channel alone is not enough: many sources can explain the same spelling, so something must express which sources are plausible words in the first place.
  3. Training, and alignment by EM. "Training" here means counting correspondences in examples. The complication is that the examples are not pre-aligned: given qatïr written with three Chinese characters, nobody has marked which character covers which sounds. Expectation–Maximisation solves this by bootstrapping — guess an alignment, count correspondences, use those counts to guess a better alignment, repeat until it stops changing. Typically eight to ten rounds here.
  4. Keying and backoff. A model keyed on the character needs many examples of that character. Keyed on the character's reconstructed reading, every character pronounced alike contributes to the same bucket. Backoff is the fallback chain when a bucket is too thin: try the full reading, else the initial-plus-vowel, else the initial alone, else the overall average. This lifted coverage of Xiongnu characters from 38% to 100%, and accuracy by 16 points.
  5. Held-out testing. The model is trained on part of the data and tested on a part it has never seen. Testing on training data measures memorisation, not ability. For the Mongolian corpus we held out whole chapters rather than random entries, because a random split leaks: neighbouring entries share names and formulae.
  6. Exact match and edit distance. "Exact" is unforgiving — one wrong sound fails the whole word. Edit distance counts how many single-sound insertions, deletions or substitutions separate the guess from the truth, so "within 2" means the reconstruction was at most two sounds off. Both are reported because exact match alone hides whether the model is close or wildly wrong.
  7. Domain shift. A model learns the statistics of its training data, including quirks specific to that source. Deployed on data from a different source, it can fail badly even though it appeared excellent in testing. This is the central reliability problem in applied machine learning generally, and it is why the same-domain result (25%) and the cross-language result (3.1%) had to be reported separately here.
  8. Comparing languages, and null models. To ask which language best explains a transcription, you score each candidate and compare — the ratio of two such scores is a Bayes factor. Each candidate needs a model of what its words look like: an n-gram model, which learns which short sound sequences that language permits. Any such comparison needs a null model — what score would arise by chance? Here that was done by permutation: pair each Chinese transcription with a randomly chosen different foreign word and re-score, several hundred times, to see where the real result falls in that distribution. And negative controls — running the method on cases whose answer is already known — are what exposed the failure. Without them the method would have produced confident, wrong answers.
  9. The name table. All 39 Xiongnu names, titles and lexical items with their Later Han readings, a Turkish-orthography pronunciation key, and an assessment of whether each reading can be reconciled with proposals in the literature. The Later Han column is not this project's output — it is taken directly from Schuessler's published reconstructions. What the table adds is the assembly: the names collected in one place, the readings joined to them character by character, and the fit to published etymologies stated openly rather than assumed.
  10. Searching a dictionary, and why it needs a null. Given a reading written out in Turkish letters, the search compares it to every dictionary entry of similar length and returns the closest. Closeness is scored on the consonant skeleton rather than the whole word, because §6 shows that is the part of the signal that survives: initials come through at 79–94%, nasal endings at 99%, vowels at 50%, and stop endings not at all. The costs follow those numbers — a voicing difference (k/g, t/d) is cheap, a missing final stop is cheap, a change of place or manner is expensive, and a vowel mismatch counts a quarter. The null model then runs the identical search on pseudo-names assembled from the same Later Han syllables in the wrong order: same shape, same sounds, no meaning. Comparing the two is what converts "that looks like a Turkish word" into a number. Without it the search has no failure mode — it returns a confident answer for any input whatsoever, including deliberate nonsense. The null is rebuilt for every lexicon, which is the only way to compare lexicons at all: a bigger word list raises the raw number of matches and the chance rate together, so counting matches without recomputing the null makes every enlargement look like an improvement.
  11. A reproducibility wrinkle worth naming. The headword count depends on which version of the PDF text-extraction library is used: two runs of the same script on the same file, on different machines, produced 6,820 and 6,880 entries — a 60-entry disagreement about where lines break in the underlying page stream. That moves the noise floor slightly (0.200 against 0.192) and nothing else: both runs put tengri fourth at 0.233, tümen third at 0.183, and three names of thirty-six above chance. The figures quoted here are from the second machine, which is the run the released data files come from. It is a small thing, but it is the kind of small thing that is invisible until someone else runs your code — which is the argument for releasing it.

The first three columns are the same evidence as those tables; the fourth is speculation and carries no support from anything measured in this paper. One pattern in it is worth singling out, because it is the only entry with independent corroboration: from 呼韓邪 onward every chanyu title ends in 若鞮, and the Hou Hanshu states that this element was added from that reign and glosses it 孝 "filial, devoted". The author reads the same element as Inakt, "the faithful ones" — a proposal that agrees with a gloss recorded by the Chinese source itself rather than with a modern dictionary, which is already a different order of evidence from the rest of this table.

The word behind it is attested. Searching the two lexicons of §3 for inaq returns Inak, glossed fidelis "faithful", in the Codex Cumanicus (Kuun 1880, p. 182).12 It is absent from Kāşgarî's index, so the word belongs to the Kipchak tradition rather than the Karakhanid one. Old Turkic ınaq "trusted one, confidant" is generally derived, with ınan- "to trust" (whence modern inanç), from a root ına- "to rely on". So the sense the proposal needs — faithful, loyal — is documented in a c. 1300 glossary, and it matches the Hou Hanshu's 孝 independently.

Two formal objections were put to this reading. One of them has now been answered by measurement, and the other has decided which form the proposal should take.

The suffix. A reading ınaq-tı is not available: -tI is not a noun-forming suffix, and the lexicons show it rather than a grammar having to assert it. Of the 31 Cuman headwords ending in -tI, almost all are glossed with Latin perfects — Ayti: dixit, Azti: transgressus est, Öwtiti: detersit — the definite past ending; the rest are numerals (Alti: sex) or Arabic loans. Kāşgarî's index has only six, none containing a suffix at all. A title cannot be a past-tense verb. But Old Turkic has a second candidate that fits exactly: -t, a plural used specifically with titles — tegin → tegit, tarqan → tarqat. On that reading the element is Inakt, "the faithful ones", and 若鞮 is not one ruler's epithet but a title carried by the whole line from 呼韓邪 onward — which is exactly how the Chinese sources use it. The six compound titles in the table are glossed on that reading, as [personal name], of the Inakt, rather than as compounds in which "faithful" modifies the name. An earlier draft did the latter and counted the faithfulness twice, once in the name and once in the title it was already attached to.

The spelling. Against Inakt it was objected that Later Han Chinese had -t final syllables and a scribe hearing a final -t would have used one, rather than the open syllable 鞮 te. That is a claim about scribal habit, so it was counted. In the Sanskrit corpus — the same period — 770 pairs align one character to one syllable; where the source syllable ends in a stop, the scribe closed it with the same stop 48% of the time and used an open character 30% of the time. Nasal codas, by contrast, were closed 83% (-n) and 73% (-ŋ) of the time. An open character after a stop is ordinary, not exceptional, so the objection does not hold.22 The nasal-versus-stop asymmetry is the same one §6 found surviving the period gap, appearing here in what the scribe chose to write.

What is still unexplained is the initial vowel: if the source was ınakt, the opening ı- is not written, and 若鞮 has no room for it. That objection stands. So the position is narrower than a claim and firmer than a guess — the sense is attested, the morphology is licensed, the spelling is normal, and one segment is unaccounted for. Clauson's entry on ınaq is where a Turkologist would take it further; src/lookup_lexicons.py and src/step20_coda_spelling.py reproduce every count quoted here.

CAppendix: research log — corrections, withdrawn readings, and how the guessing actually works

This appendix exists so that I can reconstruct later how the work was done, including the parts that were wrong. None of it appears in the submitted paper or in Supplementary S1, where the proposals are presented as they now stand rather than as a history of revisions. Here the history is the point.

C.1  The step-7 decoder cannot be quoted for an individual name

This is the most important thing in this appendix, because I made the mistake myself and it is an easy one to make again. Step 7's output file gives, for each of the 39 items, a ranked list of readings with probabilities. For 且渠 it gives Çaka at 0.583. It is tempting to read that as the model endorsing a reading. It is not.

The channel has two modes. A character can be reading-anchored, meaning the emission distribution was learned for that specific character, or it can fall back to a table keyed on the Later Han initial consonant class alone, discarding the vowel and the coda. Of the 39 items, only 5 are reading-anchored throughout — 頭曼, 烏維, 單于, 閼氏, 羯. Thirty-four use backoff on at least one character, and nine use it on every character.

The consequence is that seventeen characters of the record share one emission distribution:

velar     17 chars  稽 渠 胡 湖 壺 戶 侯 姑 孤 骨 谷 韓 諧 皋 權 朐 徑   →  ka 0.34, ga 0.15, ku 0.08
affricate  6 chars  且 粥 珠 臣 詹 郅                                  →  ca 0.18, ja 0.10, cā 0.08
dental     9 chars  鞮 題 當 屠 撑 株 稚 脫 頓                          →  ta 0.23, da 0.11, ti 0.10
liquid     6 chars  廬 攣 祿 累 蠡 閭                                  →  ra 0.37, la 0.17, rī 0.08

So 稽 kʰeiᴮ and 渠 gɨɑ get the same distribution, and 粥 tśuk and 且 tsɑᴮ get the same one. 且渠 is affricate + velar and decodes to Çaka; 稽粥 is velar + affricate and decodes to Kaça. They are mirror images of a single table entry. Çaka is not evidence about 且渠; it is what the table returns for any affricate-plus-velar string in the record.

Two further limits, both stated in step 7's own output notes and both easy to forget. The probabilities are normalised over a retained beam, so they are relative rankings within that beam and not posteriors. And the syllable inventory is Sanskrit-derived, so q and the front rounded vowels ö and ü cannot be produced at all — which means a reading such as küçük is not rejected by the decoder, it was never available to it. §5.2 of this paper makes the general point; this is the specific mechanism behind it.

Rule for later. Quote step 15's initial-correspondence counts, which are direct observation, when asking whether a spelling is permitted. Never quote a step-7 probability in support of a particular reading.

C.2  Readings I proposed and then withdrew

且渠, first read Kadı "judge". Withdrawn because kadı is an Arabic loan into Turkish and cannot stand in a Han-period title under any account. This was the weakest thing I had written down. It is now read Çaka "the striker", from the verb çak-, which is at least native — though there is no çaka headword in any lexicon, only the verb and its derivatives, and the Chinese sources do not record what the office did, so no sense can be tested.

稽粥, first read Keyçuk "the wild, untamed one". Withdrawn because the shape and the sense came from two different words. The shape fits kiçig / kiçik / kiçük "small, younger" — attested in Kāşgarî, in the Kutadgu Bilig (kiçig oğlan erken, 1823), and in the Codex Cumanicus as Kečak glossed paucus. The sense "wild" belongs to Old Turkic keyik, which has no ç in it at all. It is now read Kiçük "the younger", which also fits the succession the Chinese sources record: 稽粥 is Modu's son.

Both withdrawals were prompted by running the lexicon search rather than by reasoning about the readings, which is the general lesson: search first, then argue.

C.3  How I actually generate a proposal, and why that is a weakness

Three habits do most of the work, and it is worth being blunt about them because each one widens the search space rather than constraining it.

The epithet habit: later Turkic rulers are commonly named by a common noun or a natural force — Bayezid I Yıldırım the thunderbolt, Alp Arslan the lion, Mehmed IV Avcı the hunter, Süleyman I Kanunî the lawgiver — so I treat a reading that yields such a noun as more likely. The career habit: where the Chinese record says what a ruler did, I prefer a reading that names the deed, which risks reading the biography back into the name. The compound habit: a long transcription gets split into two or three Turkic elements, and the longer the string the more ways there are to split it — which is why 呼都而尸道皋若鞮, at eight characters, is the least constrained reading I have.

One tic worth noticing: I proposed a solar image twice, for 軍臣 (Künçin, bright as the sun) and 詹師廬 (Çamşılı, dazzling). That is either a real pattern in steppe naming or evidence that I have a favourite metaphor, and I cannot currently tell which. The same goes for the natural-force readings Gürleyen and Kayantı.

C.4  Earlier corrections worth remembering

The -t objection to Inakt. I initially argued that a scribe wanting a final -t would have used a -t-final character rather than the open 鞮. Step 20 measured it: 30% of stop-final source syllables were written with an open character. My objection dissolved, and the reading survived. This is the model for how an objection should be handled — turned into a count.

The tengri box. I first wrote it as though Georg's Yeniseian derivation settled something. It does not need to be accepted: a word attested across Turkic, Mongolic and possibly Yeniseian cannot discriminate between them either way, and that is the form the argument now takes.

The modern-echo column. An early draft carried a "proposed modern equivalent" column for every name. Almost all of it was noise, and printing it beside measured results implied a support the nulls had already denied. Removed; only the handful of significant matches are named, next to the table that measures them.
