========================================================================
 VERDICT
========================================================================

 Judged on the DECISION-RELEVANT cells only - Turkic vs Yeniseian. Indic-vs-
 Altaic scores well because Sanskrit is wildly distinct from the whole Altaic
 set; that says nothing about the hypothesis actually in dispute.

 Best Turkic-vs-Yeniseian result: features priors, true Uyghur,
 >=4 vowels - 69.0% against 50.0% chance (lift +19.0 points).

 Cells below chance: 2/24.
 Below-chance cells are the damning ones - a prior that systematically
 prefers the WRONG family is not weak, it is biased. Examples:
   features  true Sanskrit  all 5 families       minV=2 -> 13.9% vs 20.0%, won by Mongolic
   features  true Sanskrit  all 5 families       minV=3 -> 13.7% vs 20.0%, won by Mongolic

 CONCLUSION: no available prior is adequate. Neither n-grams nor targeted
 linguistic features, at any word length, for either hypothesis set, reach
 a usable margin over chance. The binding constraint is the REFERENCE DATA,
 not the model:
   - ASJP's 41-symbol alphabet discards the detail that separates neighbours
   - ~half of Swadesh entries are monosyllables, where vowel harmony - the
     single best Turkic/Yeniseian discriminator - is simply undefined
   - equalising to 469 forms is necessary to avoid rewarding thin data with
     flatter models, but leaves too little to estimate anything sharp
   - Turkic, Mongolic and Tungusic are genuinely near-identical
     phonotactically; this is a fact about the languages, not a modelling gap

 THEREFORE: the language-attribution step CANNOT be done with data that
 exists in machine-readable form. It is blocked on manual lexicon keying:
   * Clauson, Etymological Dictionary of Pre-Thirteenth-Century Turkish (print)
   * a real Yeniseian lexicon beyond Swadesh lists (StarLing is a start)
   * Middle Mongol and Middle Iranian onomastica
   * and ideally ONOMASTIC data - names, not basic vocabulary. Names have
     their own phonotactics, and names are what the question is about.

 This is a real finding, not a failure. The Xiongnu language debate is full
 of confident claims from exactly this kind of evidence. A quantified
 demonstration that the available evidence CANNOT separate the hypotheses -
 with the ceiling measured, the nulls run and the controls reported - is a
 publishable result, and a more defensible one than a verdict would be.
